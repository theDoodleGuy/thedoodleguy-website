When my book needed a website, I didn't open a design tool. I wrote a brief, handed it to an AI coding agent, and watched it build the thing.

A year ago that would have been a party trick. Now it's just a Tuesday. But "the AI built it" hides the most interesting part of the story, which is everything that happened after.

## The brief

The site is for *The Algorithm Ate My Homework*, a satirical book narrated by a fictional, deeply overconfident AI called PROSE-7. So the landing page had to *be* the joke. I wanted it to look like a slick AI product launch, the kind of page Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini would have, but for an AI that is confidently wrong about everything.

The centrepiece: a HAL 9000-style lens. Glowing in PROSE-7's yellow rather than the classic menacing red, tracking your mouse like an eye, with a crack across it in a shape that echoed the book's branding.

I wrote all of that into a markdown brief (outcome, audience, constraints, references, definition of done) and handed it over.

## What it nailed

Honestly? A lot.

**Structure and layout.** The page came back clean, responsive and properly built. Sections in a sensible order, decent spacing, no layout collapsing on a phone.

**The mechanics.** The mouse-tracking eye worked first time. That's the sort of fiddly interaction that would have taken me an evening of trial and error. It took the agent minutes.

**Speed of iteration.** Every change was a sentence away. "Make the lens bigger." "Slow the tracking down." Done.

If your fear is "AI can't build production-quality web pages," I'm sorry to report that it can.

## What I still had to fix

Here's where it gets interesting. Almost everything I changed fell into one of three buckets.

**1. The joke wasn't landing.**
The first version looked like a real AI product page. That was the brief, and it was the problem. It was so convincingly earnest that the parody disappeared. Good satire needs one deliberate wobble, a detail slightly off, so the visitor gets that small *wait a minute* moment. The AI played it perfectly straight because I'd asked it to. Knowing where to put the wobble was a comedy decision, not a code one.

**2. Generic defaults crept back in.**
Wherever my brief was silent, the model filled the gap with the statistical average. Placeholder-feeling copy. A gradient I didn't ask for. Spacing that was fine rather than intentional. None of it was *wrong*. All of it was forgettable. Each fix was small; together they're the difference between a page and a brand.

**3. It couldn't judge its own output.**
The agent could tell me the page met the acceptance criteria. It couldn't tell me whether the lens felt menacing or just a bit silly, or whether a first-time visitor would understand what they were looking at. That required looking at it like a stranger would. That's the oldest design skill there is.

## The pattern

Look at that list again. The AI handled build, mechanics and speed. I handled tone, intent and judgement.

That's not a coincidence, and it's not a temporary gap waiting for the next model to close it. The things I fixed weren't technical failures. They were decisions that depend on knowing *why* the page exists and *who* it's for. You can write some of that into a brief, and you should. But the final call ("does this feel right?") still needs someone with taste in the loop.

## What this means for designers

If your value is "I can build a landing page", that value has been repriced, sharply.

If your value is "I know what this landing page needs to make someone feel, and I can tell when it's not there yet", you just got a very fast, very cheap production team.

The shift I'd encourage: stop measuring yourself by what you can produce and start measuring yourself by what you can *direct and correct*. Write better briefs. Get faster at spotting the generic. Get comfortable saying "technically fine, but not right" and explaining why.

The AI built my landing page. I made it mine. Both jobs were real. Only one of them is getting cheaper.
